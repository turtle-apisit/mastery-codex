# Requirement Definition

## Summary

The aim of the requirements activity is **to determine the client's needs — not
what the client wants**. It begins with **concept exploration**.

**IEEE Std 610.12-1990** gives three definitions, and the trick is that each one
lives somewhere different:

1. A **condition or capability needed by a stakeholder** to solve a problem or
   achieve an objective → *lives in a **person***
2. A **condition or capability that must be met or possessed by a system** or
   system component to satisfy a contract, standard, specification, or other
   formally imposed document → *lives in the **world***
3. A **documented representation** of a condition or capability as in (1) or (2)
   → *lives in a **document***

**Two sub-activities:**

- **Requirement elicitation** (also requirement capture, requirements gathering)
  — **discovering** the client's requirements. Methods include interviews and
  surveys.
- **Requirement analysis** — **refining and extending** the initial requirements.

**Overview of the requirements activity:**

1. Gain an understanding of the **application domain** — the specific business
   environment the product will operate in
2. **Build a business model** — use UML to describe the client's business
   processes
3. Use the business model to **determine the client's requirements**
4. **Iterate** the above steps

## Key points

- The three IEEE definitions are not competing — they are the same requirement at
  three points in its life: felt by a person, imposed on a system, written in a
  document. Losing track of which one is meant is the source of a lot of
  argument.
- The activity is **explicitly iterative** (step 4), even inside a waterfall.

## Watch out for

- "Determine needs, not wants" is the single sentence this whole class hangs on.
  See [[wants-and-needs]].

None — this is a root concept for the subject.
